Flat design became dominant around 2013, when Apple flattened iOS and the rest of the industry followed. By 2016, gradients were embarrassing. By 2018, anything resembling depth, texture, or dimension in a logo read as dated.

Now gradients are back. Texture is back. Dimension is — cautiously — back. The question for any brand is: what do you follow, and why?

## The problem with chasing design cycles

A logo is not a fashion item. The brands with the most enduring identities — IBM, Penguin Books, the London Underground roundel — have evolved slowly over decades and look like themselves, not like the era they were designed in.

The brands that chased flat design hard in 2014, and are now chasing dimensionality in 2025, have logos that feel transitional. They're current but not timeless. In five years they'll need updating again.

Following trends at logo level is expensive and disorienting for audiences who've built up recognition equity.

## What's actually driving the dimensional revival

The shift isn't aesthetic nostalgia. It's technological and contextual.

AI-generated imagery, 3D rendering tools accessible to non-specialists, and the dominance of video content have all raised the visual resolution of the media environment. Flat logos disappear into increasingly complex, dimensional backdrops.

At the same time, the meaning of "digital-native" has shifted. A generation of brands used flatness to signal that they were born online, not adapted from legacy physical assets. But that generation is now the establishment. Flatness no longer signals modern; it signals mid-2010s.

## How we're approaching the question

The answer for any specific brand is contextual, not categorical. We ask:

**What formats does this logo primarily live in?** A brand that lives in video and 3D environments has different needs than one that lives primarily on packaging and print.

**What is the brand's relationship to technology?** Some brands want to signal craft and physical materiality. Dimensionality and texture serve this. Others want to signal efficiency and frictionlessness. Clean and flat still serves them.

**What is the competitive landscape doing?** If every brand in your category has gone dimensional, the minimal flat identity is now the disruptive choice.

The answer is never "follow the trend" or "resist the trend." It's "understand what the choice communicates and make it deliberately."
