We've never produced our best work from a vague brief. That might sound obvious. But the number of studios that treat the brief as a formality — something to acknowledge and then ignore — is remarkable.

The brief isn't just a document. It's the first design decision of a project. How you define the problem shapes every solution you'll ever find for it.

## What a brief actually needs to answer

Not "what do you want the logo to look like." That's not a brief question. A brief should answer:

- What does this brand need to do in the world?
- Who will encounter it, and in what contexts?
- What do competitors look like, and how should this brand relate to them — blend in, stand apart, or reframe the category?
- What can't change? (Existing equity, brand elements with legal protection, board sign-off requirements)
- What does success look like in 12 months?

## The silence problem

Clients often don't know what they want until they see what they don't want. This is completely normal human behavior, but it's also a trap for designers who treat the initial brief as final.

We build a "wrong answers only" exercise into every discovery phase. We deliberately show concepts we know the client won't like, because their reaction to the wrong answer is the clearest articulation of the right answer we can get.

The brief is a living document. It should be updated every time you learn something that changes your understanding of the problem.
