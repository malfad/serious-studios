Every year, a new generation of designers discovers that grids are "rules made to be broken." They start placing type at 37-degree angles and overlapping images in ways that feel transgressive and alive. Some of it works. Most of it doesn't.

The ones who figure out why it doesn't work usually become very good designers.

## What the grid actually is

A grid isn't a visual outcome. It's a decision-making system. Its job is to eliminate arbitrary choices so that every deviation from it carries meaning.

When everything is intentionally placed, nothing reads as intentional. The grid gives you the contrast you need to make a choice land.

Think of it like silence in music. Silence is only meaningful against sound. A grid break is only expressive against structure.

## The three-level grid system we use

We design with three levels of grid:

**1. The macro grid** — columns, margins, and the overarching spatial logic of the page or screen. This rarely breaks, and when it does, it's a major compositional event.

**2. The content grid** — the sub-divisions within the macro grid that govern where type, images, and other content elements sit. This flexes based on content needs but follows derived proportions.

**3. The micro grid** — baseline grids, icon alignment, button padding, input field sizing. This is where inconsistency kills polish. Most "unfinished" designs are unfinished at this level.

Knowing which level you're working at tells you when breaking is transgression (bad) and when it's expression (good).

## The brands that do this best

Look at how Virgil Abloh approached Off-White's visual identity. The quotation marks, the arrows, the measurements printed on the physical garments — they only worked because they existed against a rigidly structured typographic grid. The chaos was organized chaos. That's not a contradiction. It's the whole point.

Or look at how The Economist has maintained one of the most recognizable brand aesthetics in print journalism for decades. Not by being flashy. By being so structurally consistent that every slight deviation — a photograph that breaks the column, a pull-quote that bleeds to the edge — reads as editorial emphasis rather than accident.

## How to practice this

Pick a 12-column grid. Design 10 pages using it strictly. Then design three more pages where you break it once each — once dramatically, once subtly, once at the type level only.

Compare them. The breaks on pages 11-13 will be the most expressive things you've made. They'll have earned their freedom.
