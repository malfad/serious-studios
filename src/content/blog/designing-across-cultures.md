Every visual decision carries cultural assumptions. The direction text flows. The colors that signal celebration or caution. The spatial relationships that read as intimate versus formal. The typography that signals modernity in one market and illegibility in another.

Designers trained in a particular context carry those assumptions invisibly, the same way you carry an accent. You don't hear it in yourself; everyone else does.

## Where global brand systems break down

The most common failure mode: a brand identity developed in London or New York, packaged in a comprehensive global guidelines document, and handed to regional teams to "adapt for local markets."

The adaptation brief is usually: "maintain brand consistency while being locally relevant." In practice this means: keep everything the same but maybe swap the people in the photography.

It doesn't work. The problems are structural, not executional.

Color systems built on Western associations — where white signals purity and simplicity, for instance — carry completely different associations in contexts where white is the color of mourning. You can't adapt your way out of that with photography.

## The hierarchy question

Western design tends toward a specific information hierarchy: large, immediate, emotional visual; smaller, explanatory text below. This hierarchy reflects a visual culture shaped by particular advertising traditions.

In markets where text-dense communication is the norm — where more information signals more respect for the reader's intelligence — this hierarchy can read as evasive or superficial.

We've seen premium brands lose credibility in markets because their minimal-text approach read as having nothing to say, not as confident restraint.

## What good cross-cultural design practice looks like

It doesn't mean designing differently for every market. It means building flexibility into the system from the start — and doing the research to know where the system's assumptions break down before deployment, not after.

Concretely:

**Involve native perspective early** — not as a review step at the end, but in the definition of the brand character. What does "modern" mean in this market? What does "premium" look like here?

**Test the system in context** — mock it up in actual market environments. Put it on shelves with local competitors. Show it to real people, not brand managers.

**Build for typographic diversity** — if your system uses a Latin-script typeface chosen for its specific personality, you need a partner typeface for Devanagari, Arabic, or Chinese that carries the same feeling. This requires budget and specialist input. Plan for it.

**Make the adaptation rules explicit** — what can regional teams change, and what can't they? A system with clear, principled flexibility is more consistent in practice than one that says "stay on brand" without defining what that means locally.

None of this is simple. But the alternative — a global brand that reads as foreign in every market — is simpler in the short term and much more expensive in the long one.
