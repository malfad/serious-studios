Packaging design is the most brutally evaluated design work that exists. The consumer isn't sitting at their desk with time to appreciate nuance. They're pushing a trolley, probably tired, with a list in their head and a toddler nearby. Your design has to register, communicate, and persuade in less than a third of a second.

Most packaging fails this test not because it looks bad — it often looks beautiful in isolation — but because it was designed in isolation.

## The shelf context problem

The most common mistake we see when reviewing packaging briefs: the reference images are all individual product shots on white backgrounds. The product will never be seen on a white background. It will be seen next to 12 competitors, under fluorescent light, at a slight angle, while the shopper is moving.

We call this the shelf context problem. You're designing for photography, not for retail.

Our process starts with competitive shelf mapping — we photograph or mock up the actual shelf environment and design within it, not separately from it.

## The hierarchy of shelf impact

There are four things a package must communicate, in strict priority order:

1. **What is it?** — Category recognition before brand recognition
2. **Who made it?** — Brand identification
3. **Why this one?** — Key differentiator (taste, benefit, value)
4. **How to use it?** — The how, only if relevant at point of purchase

Most packaging tries to answer all four at equal volume. The result is visual noise that answers nothing. Ruthless hierarchy — deciding what gets silence so something else can speak — is the hardest design skill in packaging.

## Color and the attention system

Human vision is tuned to contrast and movement. On a static shelf, you have contrast to work with.

High contrast packaging — deep blacks, stark whites, sharp complementary color pairings — grabs attention first. Muted, tonal, sophisticated palettes read as premium but need to earn attention through shape and structure rather than color contrast.

Neither approach is wrong. They're different tools for different brand positionings. What's wrong is choosing your approach based on what you find beautiful at your desk.

## The five-meter test

Before anything goes to client presentation, we do the five-meter test: we print the design, put it on a wall, and walk away to five meters. If the brand and category are still readable, we continue. If they aren't, we go back.

It sounds crude. It catches more problems than any other review step we've introduced.
