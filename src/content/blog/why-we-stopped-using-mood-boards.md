We used mood boards for years. Everyone does. You pull references, arrange them in a grid, show the client "the direction" — and they either love it or send you back to Pinterest.

The problem crept up slowly. We started noticing that mood board reviews were becoming the most contentious part of any project. Clients who had been aligned on positioning and brand character would fragment the moment images appeared. One stakeholder loves the Nordic minimalism references; another wants something "warmer, more human." 

The images were doing the wrong work.

## What mood boards actually do

A mood board is a shortcut for communicating aesthetic direction. That's genuinely useful — but only when the aesthetic direction has been correctly derived from strategic decisions.

When you show a mood board before that work is complete, you're asking clients to approve an output before agreeing on the inputs. And clients — understandably — respond to what they see emotionally. They can't do anything else. They haven't been given the tools to evaluate whether the aesthetic serves the strategy.

The result: you iterate on aesthetics while the strategic questions stay open. The project gets longer, more expensive, and harder to defend at each stage.

## The replacement: brand direction documents

We replaced mood boards with what we call a Brand Direction Document — a one-page brief that establishes:

- **Brand character in three words** — chosen from a structured exercise, not a free-for-all
- **Tone poles** — two spectrums (e.g. "serious ↔ playful" and "heritage ↔ modern") with an agreed position on each
- **One example brand** (ideally outside the category) that embodies the desired feeling
- **One anti-example** — what the brand must not feel like, and why

Only when this document is signed off do we move to visual territory.

## What we gained

The shift changed the nature of our design reviews. When a client questions a choice now, we can point back to the Brand Direction Document: "This feels cold to you — but we agreed the brand lives at the serious end of the spectrum. Let's talk about whether that decision was right, not whether this execution is wrong."

That's a productive conversation. The old version — "I don't know, I just don't love the vibe" — wasn't.

We still use visual references. We just use them to *illustrate* decisions already made, not to make decisions that haven't been made yet.
