When we brand a product, we're designing a static object — or close to it. The bottle doesn't change once it's on the shelf. The logo on the label is the same at 9am as it is at 9pm.

Hospitality brands don't work like this. A restaurant brand is experienced across a reservation confirmation, an exterior sign at dusk, a menu at candlelight, a napkin, a plate, a bill, and a Google review the next morning. The brand has to hold together across all of those — and it has to do it in time, not just space.

## The sensory dimensions most brands ignore

A hotel has a scent. A restaurant has a sound. These aren't ancillary details. They're core brand elements that persist in memory long after visual elements have faded.

We now include sensory direction in every hospitality brief we write: what does this space smell like? What's the sonic texture — not a playlist, but an acoustic character? How does it feel underfoot?

These questions aren't soft. When a luxury hotel brand has a beautifully designed identity but smells like generic cleaning product and has a playlist algorithm playing the same mid-tempo electronica as every other hotel, the brand doesn't land. The visual work is undermined by everything the eyes can't see.

## The staff problem

The most underrated element of a hospitality brand is the people who deliver it.

We've worked on properties where the visual identity is exceptional — refined typography, considered materials, beautiful photography — but where no one on the front desk has ever seen the brand guidelines. The welcome you receive is warm but generic. The brand promise made by the design is broken within 30 seconds of arrival.

This is a brief problem, not a design problem. When we take on hospitality projects now, we insist on a conversation about internal brand culture as part of the engagement. We can't control hiring or training. But we can define, in the brand language, what the guest experience is supposed to feel like — and give the people responsible for it a vocabulary to work with.

## Designing for memory, not approval

In most brand reviews, you're designing for approval — does the client like this? Does it look good in the presentation?

In hospitality, you're designing for memory. Will a guest remember this detail in six months, and will that memory bring them back or bring a friend?

That's a different design question. It often leads to restraint rather than elaboration — one truly considered material, one piece of writing that lands, one gesture that feels like it couldn't belong anywhere else. The accumulation of those specifics is what people remember. The generic parts, however beautiful, dissolve.
